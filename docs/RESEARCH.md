# Research Compilation — Bill C-3 Canadian Citizenship by Descent

## Legislative Timeline

- **December 19, 2023**: Ontario Superior Court of Justice declares parts of the Citizenship Act (first-generation limit) unconstitutional under Section 15 of the Charter (equality rights)
- **June 5, 2025**: Government introduces Bill C-3, An Act to Amend the Citizenship Act (2025)
- **November 5, 2025**: Bill C-3 passes the House of Commons
- **November 20, 2025**: Royal Assent
- **December 15, 2025**: Bill C-3 comes into force — IRCC begins applying new rules

## What Bill C-3 Does (Summary)

Bill C-3 amends the Citizenship Act to:
1. Remove the first-generation limit on citizenship by descent for people born before Dec 15, 2025 (retroactive)
2. Allow citizenship to flow beyond the first generation for future births, subject to a "substantial connection" test (1,095 days physical presence)
3. Extend citizenship to remaining "Lost Canadians" and their descendants
4. Provide a simplified renunciation process for those who gained citizenship automatically but don't want it
5. Enable direct grant of citizenship for children adopted abroad by Canadian parents in the second or later generation

## Scale of Impact

- Estimated 350,000 to 500,000 individuals worldwide may become newly eligible
- Largest affected populations: United States, United Kingdom, Australia
- Significant interest from Americans with Canadian ancestry — waves of Canadian emigration to the US throughout 19th and 20th centuries (French Canadians to New England, Maritimers to Boston, cross-border migration to Detroit, Buffalo, Seattle)

## Application Process — Key Facts

### Paper vs Online
- IRCC's online portal was designed for the former first-generation limit
- Multi-generational claims (G2+) are better suited to paper applications
- Paper applications allow inclusion of complex lineage documents and cover letters
- IRCC may accept straightforward online applications for simple cases
- When in doubt: submit paper

### Processing Times (as of early 2026)
- Average: ~10 months
- IRCC 2026-2027 target: 80% of citizenship grant applications within 12 months
- 96% meeting quality management program standards
- Multi-generational claims may take longer than first-generation claims
- Surge in applications following Bill C-3 has created processing backlogs

### The Application is for "Proof of Citizenship"
- Applicants are NOT applying for a grant of citizenship
- They are requesting proof of citizenship they already hold by operation of law
- Citizenship is recognized retroactively — it existed since birth
- No deadline to apply
- No residency requirement for the application itself

## The Substantial Connection Test (Prospective Only)

Applies to: children born or adopted abroad ON OR AFTER December 15, 2025, to a Canadian parent also born or adopted abroad.

**Requirement**: The Canadian parent must demonstrate at least 1,095 days (3 years) of cumulative physical presence in Canada at any time before the child's birth or adoption.

**Acceptable evidence:**
- Employment records, T4 slips, pay stubs
- Academic transcripts, school enrollment
- Provincial health cards, immunization records
- Rental agreements, utility bills
- Bank statements
- Passport stamps, CBSA entry/exit records

**Important**: Days do NOT need to be consecutive.

## Chain of Descent — How It Works

Under Bill C-3 (retroactive), citizenship flows through the entire chain:

**Example — G3 claim (great-grandparent as anchor):**
- G0 (Great-grandparent): Born in Canada → Canadian citizen
- G1 (Grandparent): Born abroad → retroactively recognized as citizen under Bill C-3
- G2 (Parent): Born abroad → retroactively recognized as citizen
- G3 (Applicant): Born abroad → retroactively recognized as citizen

**Key principle**: You don't skip generations. Your parent must be recognized as a citizen (even retroactively) before citizenship flows to you. But Bill C-3 handles this automatically — the retroactive recognition flows through the entire chain.

**Important**: Ancestors in the chain do NOT need to have applied for or held citizenship certificates. The applicant documents the chain and IRCC recognizes the status retroactively.

## Historical Citizenship Law Context

Understanding why this is complex requires knowing how Canadian citizenship law has changed:

- **Before 1947**: No separate Canadian citizenship — Canadians were British subjects
- **January 1, 1947**: Canadian Citizenship Act comes into force — first standalone citizenship
- **Pre-1977 gender discrimination**: Canadian women who married foreign nationals couldn't always pass citizenship to children born abroad (men could)
- **1977 amendments**: Addressed some gender-based discrimination
- **April 17, 2009**: First-generation limit imposed — citizenship by descent limited to one generation born abroad
- **2009 and 2015 amendments**: Restored citizenship to ~20,000 "Lost Canadians"
- **December 15, 2025**: Bill C-3 removes the first-generation limit, addresses remaining Lost Canadians

## Quebec Ancestry — Special Considerations

### Civil Registration History

Quebec has a unique vital records system that creates significant challenges for Bill C-3 applicants:

- **1621–1993**: Births were recorded by churches (primarily Catholic parish registers), not by the government. The "acte de baptême" (baptismal record) served as the official record of birth. Registers were kept in duplicate — one at the parish, one at the local courthouse.
- **1926**: Quebec created a mechanism for non-religious civil registration, but it was rarely used.
- **January 1, 1994**: The Directeur de l'état civil (DEQ) was established as the sole civil registrar under the new Civil Code of Quebec. All births from this date forward are government-registered.
- **1994–2000s**: The DEQ undertook a massive project to transfer older church records into the civil register, focusing primarily on records from ~1900 onward. Transfer was incomplete for very old records.

### Obtaining Quebec Birth Records

1. **Directeur de l'état civil (DEQ)** — etatcivil.gouv.qc.ca. Request a "copie d'acte de naissance" (long-form equivalent). ~$20 CAD, 4-8 weeks by mail.
2. **Parish churches and diocesan archives** — If DEQ cannot locate the record. Major dioceses: Québec, Montréal, Trois-Rivières, Sherbrooke, Chicoutimi, Rimouski. ~$15-$50 CAD.
3. **BAnQ** — Holds the Drouin Collection (virtually all Quebec parish registers 1621–~1940). Research resource, not a source of certified copies.
4. **FamilySearch.org** (free) and **Ancestry.ca** (paid) — Digitized Drouin Collection. Use for research to identify parish and dates, then order certified copies from DEQ or the parish.

### IRCC and Quebec Baptismal Certificates

- IRCC's general rule: long-form birth certificate required, baptismal certificates not accepted.
- For Quebec pre-1994: the DEQ-issued "copie d'acte de naissance" is the equivalent and accepted without question.
- If DEQ has no record: submit the church baptismal certificate + DEQ negative search letter + cover letter explaining Quebec's civil registration history.
- Immigration lawyers recommend proactively explaining the Quebec system to IRCC officers who may not be familiar with it.

### Quebec Name Challenges

- **Dit names**: "Lafleur dit Beaulieu" — families used alternate surnames; descendants may use either.
- **Anglicization**: "Leblanc" → "White", "Boisvert" → "Greenwood", "Roy" → "King"
- **Spelling variation**: Pre-20th century records have rampant inconsistency ("Pelletier"/"Peltier"/"Péletier")
- **Women's names**: Quebec records use maiden names (nom de naissance), helpful for tracing lineage
- **Latin records**: Very old parish records (pre-1800s) may be in Latin, requiring certified translation

### Key Tips for Quebec Claims

- French-language documents do NOT need translation for IRCC (French is an official language of Canada)
- Latin documents DO need certified translation
- Always try the DEQ first, even for very old births — their negative search letter is itself a useful supporting document
- The Drouin Collection on FamilySearch.org is the best starting point for research
- Consider hiring a Quebec-based professional genealogist for complex cases (FQSG and SGCF can provide referrals)

## Common Questions from Reddit / Community

### "Can I claim through a grandparent?"
Yes, but indirectly. Citizenship can't skip a generation. Bill C-3 retroactively restores your parent's citizenship, which then flows to you.

### "Does my parent need to apply first?"
No. You document the chain yourself on your CIT 0001 application. IRCC doesn't require citizenship certificates for your parent or other ancestors — just birth certificates showing the parent-child relationships.

### "What if my parent/grandparent is dead?"
Death does NOT break the chain. As long as the person would have qualified under the new law, their citizenship is recognized retroactively. Provide a death certificate to help establish identity/timeline.

### "What if my ancestor never knew they were Canadian?"
Doesn't matter. Citizenship is recognized by operation of law regardless of whether the person ever claimed it.

### "Is there a generational limit?"
For people born before December 15, 2025: NO. There is no legal ceiling on how many generations back you can go. However, documentary challenges increase significantly with each generation.

### "Paper or online?"
Paper for most Bill C-3 claims (G2+). Online only for straightforward first-generation cases.

### "How long will it take?"
~10 months as of early 2026. Potentially longer for complex multi-generational cases.

### "Do I owe Canadian taxes?"
Generally no if you continue to reside outside Canada. Canada taxes based on residency, not citizenship. But consult a cross-border tax advisor for your specific situation.

### "Will this affect my US citizenship?"
Canada allows dual/multiple citizenship. The US also generally allows it, though there are nuances. This app should not provide specific legal advice on dual citizenship — recommend consulting a lawyer.

## Notes for App Development

### Accuracy is paramount
- This is a legal/governmental process. Incorrect guidance could cost someone months of processing time or lead to a rejected application.
- All factual claims in the app should be traceable to official IRCC sources.
- Legal nuances should be flagged — the app provides guidance, not legal advice.

### Tone
- Empathetic and encouraging — many users are anxious about a complex process
- Plain language — avoid legalese wherever possible
- When legal terms must be used, provide clear inline definitions
- Celebratory when appropriate — many people are discovering an identity connection they didn't know they had

### Localization considerations
- Primary audience: English-speaking, US-based
- Phase 2: French language version (required for true Canadian bilingual support)
- Currency: All fees in CAD, note exchange rate implications for non-Canadian applicants
- Date formats: Be explicit (December 15, 2025, not 12/15/25 or 15/12/25)
